Genetic evidence for globe bladderpod conservation

How regional genetic structure can guide conservation and translocation of endangered globe bladderpod.

Conservation genetics · Physaria globosa

When should we move plants—and mix populations?

Genetic mixing may help vulnerable populations, but the choice of source populations matters.

Our range-wide study of endangered globe bladderpod found two major regional genetic groups and uneven genetic vulnerability among populations. The findings support protecting natural populations while using carefully planned translocations to complement habitat conservation.

A rare plant of rocky river bluffs

Physaria globosa is a federally endangered mustard found in separated river systems in Indiana, Kentucky, and Tennessee. It grows on rocky bluffs and steep slopes, often on calcareous substrates, and produces yellow spring flowers followed by rounded fruits.

Habitat degradation, erosion, and encroachment by woody plants—including invasive shrubs—threaten these populations. Their scattered distribution raises a practical question: should conservation maintain regional differences, restore connectivity, or do both?

Regional history matters—but risk is local

Two major genetic groups

Multiple analyses supported a northern Indiana–Kentucky group and a southern Tennessee group, with limited connectivity between them. Additional structure occurs within these regions.

Uneven genetic vulnerability

Some populations retain substantial variation; others show reduced diversity, recent bottlenecks, or elevated inbreeding. Conservation needs therefore differ among populations.

Six populations, including one translocated population, showed evidence of recent bottlenecks. After accounting for null alleles in the markers, most populations showed little inbreeding, but several western Tennessee populations retained elevated estimates. These results argue for targeted monitoring rather than assuming that all populations face the same genetic risks.

What can existing translocations teach us?

Both translocated populations showed genetic mixing, but their histories and outcomes differed. They also contained few private alleles—variants unique to a sampled population—highlighting the challenge of representing natural diversity in new populations.

Mixing within a region

TR_01 was established from two Kentucky populations. Its genetic composition matched those sources, and it showed no detected bottleneck. Only a subset of sampled plants showed admixed ancestry; differences in introduction year and flowering schedules may have limited interbreeding.

Mixing across regions

TR_02 combined ancestry from the northern and southern groups. It demonstrated that plants from these regions can produce viable offspring, but it also showed a recent bottleneck and a deficit of rare alleles.

Mixing is not the same as demonstrated genetic rescue

The presence of mixed ancestry does not establish improved survival, reproduction, or long-term persistence. Controlled experiments are needed to test offspring performance and the consequences of crossing historically differentiated populations.

Conservation guided by population history

  1. Protect natural populations across the range. Maintain representation within Indiana, Kentucky, eastern Tennessee, and western Tennessee, alongside habitat management and invasive-shrub control.
  2. Prioritize the most vulnerable populations. Combine genetic indicators with demographic monitoring to identify where habitat restoration or augmentation may be warranted.
  3. Choose translocation sources deliberately. Favor genetically informed mixing among populations within the same major regional group, especially where there is evidence of natural or recent gene flow.
  4. Track outcomes after intervention. Document seed sources, retain broad genetic representation, consider flowering schedules, and monitor genetic and demographic change.

The next test: adaptation and fitness

Neutral microsatellite markers reveal structure and diversity, but do not directly measure adaptive differences or the fitness consequences of genetic mixing. Common-garden experiments, demographic data, and genome-scale analyses are needed to determine which interventions improve long-term persistence.
